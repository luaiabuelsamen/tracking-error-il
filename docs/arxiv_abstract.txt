The ACT configuration studied here exposes one timestep of measured joint positions to the policy. We show that appending the servo tracking error, the difference between the last applied position command and the current measured position, changes what a policy can learn at a 50-demonstration budget. On a physical SO-101, after a fixed demonstration replay, three training seeds were trained and evaluated in paired trials. Tracking error wins each of the two completed 20-pair comparisons, 9 vs 5 (exact McNemar p=0.29) and 14 vs 1 (p=0.001); the third evaluation was interrupted by a gripper fault after six pairs. Pooled, the completed seeds place 23 of 40 against 6 of 40 (post hoc stratified exact test p=0.0005). In simulation over five seeds, placement rises from 6.2% to 21.8%, and a position-history control recovers most of that gain, consistent with a benefit from temporal information without establishing a preferred representation. The signal requires no added sensor: it is reconstructible from logs with aligned commands and positions, and it keeps varying where the servo's load register saturates at a configured torque limit in 16.5% of demonstration frames. Across 546 archived teleoperation episodes, a preregistered prediction that larger tracking error at grasp onset accompanies a gripper-retention proxy fails (pooled d=-0.39). The study motivates reporting observation history, applied-command semantics, and actuator limits as experimental choices.
